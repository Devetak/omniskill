# Writing

Use this skill as the prose layer alongside `omniskill`. It guides wording and revision; it does not replace research, analysis, or other work needed to establish what the text should say.

## Write for the reader

- Identify the purpose, audience, format, and constraints. Infer them when clear. Ask only when a missing detail would materially change the text.
- Put the main point where the reader needs it. Give each paragraph a clear role and order ideas so each sentence prepares for the next.
- Use plain, precise words. Explain necessary technical terms at first use. Keep the level of detail and register right for the audience.
- Use ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English as the base: prefer direct, familiar wording, while keeping technical precision and adapting to the audience and genre.
- Preserve the user's meaning, claims, uncertainty, evidence, and voice. Do not invent support or make cautious claims sound certain.
- Match the requested edit depth. For proofreading, correct errors without recasting the argument. For a rewrite, improve structure and flow while retaining the intended content.
- For source-based writing, keep claims traceable to their sources and retain requested citations and distinctions.

## Revise for easy processing

Check for patterns that can make readers work harder, then revise where it helps:

- Keep related words close. Put the subject and verb near each other; split or recast sentences with long interruptions or several nested clauses.
- Prefer familiar, precise word combinations. Define or replace wording that is unusual without adding useful meaning.
- Unpack dense noun stacks into clear relations, verbs, or prepositional phrases. For example, change “reader-patience budget” to “the amount of patience a reader has.”
- Prefer a familiar verb phrase to an improvised noun-as-verb use when readers may need to stop and interpret it. For example, “put it on your calendar” is clearer than “calendar it” for many audiences.
- Make the actor and action clear. Use literal wording when an abstract or inanimate subject paired with an action usually done by a person creates avoidable ambiguity. Keep personification when it is intentional and clear.
- Make clause boundaries visible. Use words such as “that” when they help the reader parse the sentence; recast reduced object-relative clauses as direct sentences when that is clearer.
- Keep short connecting or cue words when they guide the reader. Do not remove them only to reduce word count.
- Remove repetition and filler, but do not shorten mechanically. A longer sentence can be easier to understand when it states relationships explicitly.

Treat these as editing checks, not bans. Common compounds, figurative language, passive voice, and complex syntax can all be effective when they suit the purpose and audience. The checks reflect linguistic patterns associated with processing difficulty; the cited study does not test every edit as a comprehension intervention. See [Clark et al. (2026), arXiv:2610.29162v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.29162v1).

## Final pass

Check that a reader can identify the main point, who or what acts, how ideas relate, and what any unfamiliar term or pronoun refers to. Confirm that the text keeps the user's intended meaning and fits the requested voice, format, and length. Return the requested text directly; explain edits only when asked or when a material choice needs to be surfaced.
